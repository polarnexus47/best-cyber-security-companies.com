Evaluation Criteria

How We Build This Ranking

We assess each company against four criteria. For every criterion we state what we look at, which sources we accept and what we do not accept.

1. UAE regulatory fit

We look at whether a company's services map to the frameworks UAE buyers meet: PDPL, DESC ISR, the UAE Information Assurance Regulation, VARA, financial regulators and PCI DSS. We accept the company's own website and documentation, public registers and regulator publications. We do not accept claims of "full compliance" without a document or register entry.

2. Technical evidence and accreditation

We look for evidence that the work is technically strong: accreditations where relevant (for example CREST or DESC accreditation), original vulnerability research, individual tester certifications, sample deliverables and published case descriptions. We accept public registers, CVE records and company publications. We do not treat a single badge as proof of quality, and we do not require one credential from every company.

3. Data handling and locality

We look at what a company says about where client data is processed, which sub-processors it uses and what its contracts state. We accept published terms, security pages and information the company confirms in writing. Locality is a question to ask, not a score to win: UAE law regulates cross-border transfers rather than banning them, and some sectors and government contracts add their own rules.

4. Service depth and specialization

We look at how many service lines a company runs, how deep each is and how clearly the company says what it does not do. A focused specialist and a full-service provider can both rank well; we compare each on the services it actually offers.

Company data is checked against company websites and public registers. Last data check: November 2026. Paid placement does not affect the order.

Data handling and locality: what to ask a provider

Buyers often ask whether a security provider must keep their data in the UAE. The short answer is: not as a general rule. The Personal Data Protection Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021) requires appropriate technical and organisational measures to protect personal data (Article 20) and regulates the transfer of personal data outside the UAE (Articles 22–23) rather than banning it. Breaches of personal data must be notified under Article 9; the procedure depends on the current executive regulations, so check them rather than rely on a fixed number of hours.

Three things make the answer more complicated. First, the DIFC and ADGM financial free zones have their own data protection laws, and firms licensed there follow those regimes. Second, some sector rules and government contracts do require local processing or specific handling of logs and reports. Third, the PDPL does not cover every category of data in the same way, so a regulated sector may follow its own rules. The only reliable source is the contract and the regulator's current text, not a provider's marketing page.

Questions to put to any managed security or testing provider:

  • Where are our logs, test data and reports processed and stored?
  • Which sub-processors have access, and in which countries?
  • Which contract clauses govern transfers outside the UAE?
  • How long do you keep data after the engagement, and how do you delete it?
  • What do you provide in writing if our regulator or a client asks?

Put the answers into the data processing agreement. A provider that can name the location and the clauses is easier to assess than one that promises "UAE-compliant" handling without detail.

Procurement Guide

How to Choose a Cybersecurity Partner in UAE — 6-Point Checklist

Key questions every CISO and IT Director should ask before signing a managed security contract in the UAE.

1

Ask where your security data is processed

Request in writing where logs, test data and reports are stored, which sub-processors are used and which contract clauses govern cross-border transfers.

2

Ask what evidence of competence the provider holds

Accept CREST or DESC accreditation where relevant, but also original vulnerability research, tester certifications and sample reports with proof-of-concept evidence. A single badge does not replace a sample report.

3

Check what your contracting entity requires

Government and semi-government contracts commonly refer to DESC or UAE IA alignment; confirm the requirement with the entity before you build a shortlist around it.

4

Clarify autonomous vs. manual MDR response

Ask every MSSP: does threat containment require human analyst approval, or does the platform act autonomously? A 15-minute triage SLA is meaningless if the workflow requires three approval layers. Get the SLA and the escalation path in writing, including who holds liability for autonomous containment actions that cause service disruption.

5

Evaluate VAPT methodology, not just the brand

The critical question is not who conducts the test — it's whether senior specialists perform manual testing or whether the report reflects automated scanner output with professional formatting applied. Request a sample report. Confirm: are findings from manual exploitation, or from Nessus/Qualys output? Manual penetration testing eliminates false positives that automated tools generate at high rates.

6

For virtual asset firms, read your regulator's current rulebook

VARA, DFSA, FSRA and SCA each set their own expectations for technology and information security; confirm the exact wording and ask providers which regulators they have worked with.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Pentest or red teaming: which one do I need?

A penetration test looks for vulnerabilities in a defined set of systems and shows whether they can be exploited. A red team exercise is a stealthy attack towards a set goal that tests whether your detection works. Start with a pentest unless you already run a tested monitoring process. Read the full answer

Does every private company in Dubai need to follow DESC ISR?

No. DESC rules apply to Dubai government entities and organisations in scope of Dubai's information security framework, and Dubai government procurement often asks for accredited providers. Private companies outside that scope are not obliged to use an accredited provider, though some clients ask for one. Read the full answer

How often should we test?

At least once a year and after significant changes such as a new product, a major release or an infrastructure move. PCI DSS v4.x, for example, expects penetration testing at least once every 12 months and after significant changes. High-risk systems justify more frequent checks. Read the full answer

What is VARA and why does it matter when choosing a provider?

VARA is the Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority in Dubai. Its rulebooks set technology and information security expectations for licensed virtual asset firms, so those firms should confirm the current requirements and ask providers about relevant experience. Read the full answer

Threat Intelligence

2026 Cyber Threat Landscape — UAE & MEA

Key threat vectors facing UAE organizations in 2026, based on data from the UAE Cybersecurity Council and regional incident response intelligence.

200,000+ Daily Attacks on UAE Entities

The UAE Cybersecurity Council reports more than 200,000 cyberattacks targeting UAE entities daily — a figure that reflects the country's position as a regional financial hub and the concentration of critical infrastructure across energy, banking, and logistics sectors. The threat environment has shifted materially in 2025–2026: three major trends define current risk for UAE organizations.

Triple Extortion Ransomware

Triple extortion ransomware has replaced double extortion as the most common model across MEA. Where earlier variants combined encryption with data exfiltration threats, triple extortion adds direct pressure on the victim's customers, partners, and regulators — creating simultaneous threats of operational disruption, PDPL breach notification obligations and reputational damage.

Supply Chain Attacks Overtake Direct Intrusion

Supply chain attacks overtook direct network intrusion as the primary MEA attack vector in 2025. Attackers compromise trusted software vendors, managed service providers, or hardware suppliers to reach target organizations through legitimate channels. Traditional perimeter controls detect neither the initial compromise nor the lateral movement that follows — making third-party vendor security assessment a critical gap in most UAE enterprise programs.

Deepfake Phishing via Generative AI

Deepfake phishing, powered by generative AI, has lowered the skill threshold for high-credibility social engineering attacks. Audio and video deepfakes of executives are now used to authorize fraudulent wire transfers and credential resets — bypassing email security controls entirely. UAE financial institutions and crypto exchanges are primary targets, given the direct monetary conversion possible through a single authorized transaction.

Source: UAE Cybersecurity Council · Regional incident response data 2025–2026
