Crypto and Virtual Assets Security FAQ for the UAE

Editorial Team, Cyber Vendor Research · Published: November 2026 · Updated: November 2026

Crypto firms in the UAE face two security questions: how to keep platforms and wallets safe, and what to do when funds move where they should not. These eight answers cover the regulators, what blockchain forensics delivers and how to pick a provider. Back to the FAQ hub and the regulations FAQ.

Regulators

What is VARA?

VARA is the Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority of Dubai. It licenses and supervises virtual asset service providers operating in Dubai outside the DIFC financial free zone, and publishes rulebooks that include technology and information security expectations. A firm that holds a VARA licence, or plans to apply, should read the current rulebooks and plan independent security testing in line with them. Licensed firms should also check which obligations apply to their specific activity, because exchanges, custodians and advisory services can face different rules.

Who falls under VARA, and who answers to another regulator?

It depends on where the firm is licensed and what it does. VARA covers Dubai outside DIFC, the DFSA regulates financial services in DIFC, the FSRA does so in ADGM, and the Securities and Commodities Authority acts at federal level. A firm may meet more than one regime, for example a Dubai-licensed exchange that also serves clients elsewhere. Confirm your licence perimeter with a lawyer, then check the security expectations in each rulebook. Some firms operate across zones, so one product can touch more than one regulator. Map each legal entity separately and record which regulator supervises it.

Which regulators supervise crypto firms in the UAE?

VARA in Dubai outside DIFC, DFSA in DIFC, FSRA in ADGM and SCA at federal level, with the Central Bank of the UAE overseeing payment and banking aspects. Each issues its own rules. When you brief a security provider, state which regulator you answer to so that the scope and the report match the right rulebook. Keep a short table of your legal entities, licences and supervising regulators; it saves time whenever you brief a security provider or a lawyer.

Blockchain forensics

What is blockchain forensics?

Blockchain forensics follows crypto assets across public ledgers. Specialists reconstruct the sequence of transactions, group addresses that appear to be controlled by the same party and note where funds reached services with a known owner, such as exchanges. The output is a documented reconstruction that lawyers and compliance teams can use. It states how confident each link is; it does not promise that assets can be recovered. Reports are most useful when written for non-specialists: a lawyer or a compliance officer should be able to follow the sequence of events without reading raw blockchain data.

When does a firm need blockchain forensics?

After theft from a hot wallet, an exploit, a phishing or social engineering loss, an insider case or a dispute about where funds went. It also supports compliance work: screening incoming transfers and investigating suspicious activity. A platform that holds customer assets benefits from having a provider and a process agreed before an incident, because the first hours decide how much evidence survives. Document the incident as soon as it is noticed: wallet addresses, transaction hashes, timestamps, screenshots and messages. Early, organised material saves days of reconstruction.

What are transaction tracing and wallet analysis?

Transaction tracing follows a specific movement of funds from address to address, including through bridges, swaps and mixers where the evidence allows. Wallet analysis studies the history of an address: its counterparties, timing and patterns. Together they build the graph of transactions that appears in a forensic report. The quality depends on the data sources, the networks covered and how clearly the report separates confirmed facts from likely links. The limits are real. Funds that pass through mixers or privacy-focused tools can be hard to follow, and a competent provider says where the evidence ends and the assumptions begin.

Compliance and providers

What is the FATF Travel Rule and how does it apply in the UAE?

The Travel Rule is a Financial Action Task Force standard that asks virtual asset service providers to exchange originator and beneficiary information for transfers above a threshold of USD/EUR 1,000. In the UAE it is implemented through the national anti-money-laundering framework and regulator requirements, so the exact obligations depend on your licence. Confirm the current rule with your regulator and ask providers whether they can support transaction screening in the compliance process. Transaction monitoring tools and forensic services can help build screening, but legal responsibility for compliance stays with the licensed firm.

How do I choose a provider for a crypto incident?

Ask which chains and token types the provider can analyse, how it follows funds through bridges and mixers, and how the report distinguishes facts from assumptions. Ask how it preserves evidence and documents the chain of custody, who leads the case and what the first 72 hours look like. For platform security rather than forensics, look for providers that test wallets, smart contracts and APIs manually. One example of a provider with a blockchain forensics line is Paranoid Security. Ask for a redacted sample report and check that its format suits your legal counsel and your regulator.
